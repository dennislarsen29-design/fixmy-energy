# Flagging HVAC and pool loads, not faults

Green Button interval data can support **a ranked, evidence-shown "worth an HVAC check" or "your pool pump runs at the wrong hours" flag, but not a fault diagnosis**, and for NEM homes the hard limit is solar masking: daytime net data hides house load, so only night and evening hours give a clean read. For air conditioning, 15-minute data shows weather-driven shape (afternoon ramp, cooling slope, baseload) but not compressor cycling, so short-cycling, refrigerant and airflow problems are invisible; I found no independent peer-reviewed accuracy figure for detecting any of them from whole-home 15-minute data. Pool pumps are the better-behaved target because they are long, flat, scheduled blocks: detection of filter-pump consumption in 15-minute data reached 94.8% accuracy but only 68.5% precision in one study, so roughly one in three flags would be wrong without a second check. Schedule anomalies (24/7 running, running 4-9 pm, a step change in run hours) are defensible findings; motor wear, clogs and leaks are not. Every number in our current A/C rules (2.5x ratio, 3+ spike days) is our own heuristic with no literature behind it, so we should present them as tuning parameters, tier wording by evidence, and keep the output a neutral recommendation to see a licensed contractor. Almost all evidence below comes from search summaries, not full-text reads, and is labeled as such; nothing here is legal advice.

## What a 15-minute meter can and cannot see in an air conditioner

The honest ceiling is peer-style and weather-normalized screening. A patent on smart-meter HVAC malfunction detection works by disaggregating load, comparing each home to peers in its subgroup, and flagging homes consistently in the top 1% of consumption ([US patent 11092953](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11092953)). A generic statistical outlier rule is a point beyond 2 standard deviations of a weather regression line ([EnergyCAP](https://helpcenter.energycap.com/um/cost_avoidance/information/how_to_determine_if_a_meter_is_weather_sensitive)). The better fault-detection evidence uses thermostat runtime rather than meter data: a 2021 paper compared thermostat histories from nearly 370,000 US systems to find efficiency-affecting faults ([Purdue IHPBC](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/ihpbc/378)). A study on Dataport data ran disaggregation at 1-minute, 15-minute and hourly resolution and reported 86% accuracy at 1 minute for classifying "leaky" homes, but that is a downstream task and the 15-minute result was not visible in the summary I could read ([NSF PAR 10073172](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10073172)).

Resolution is the reason. Utility data at 15-minute or hourly intervals is generally not specific enough for most published event-based disaggregation techniques to produce a clear appliance signature ([US patent 11036189](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11036189)), and a test of NILM tools found an AC with a fan-only state after compressor shutoff fit models poorly, with AC results worse than other appliances ([NILMTK](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1404.3878)). My inference is that a 15-minute reading averages many on-off cycles, so a short-cycling unit looks like a unit at partial load; cycle length, inrush and compressor-versus-fan states are not recoverable. What does survive is the slope of load against outdoor temperature, the temperature at which cooling begins, and overnight or late-evening use on hot days. LBNL's BETTER tool supports the standard 1P/3P/5P change-point models (baseload, cooling slope, change-point temperature), though it targets bills and building data ([BETTER docs](https://better-lbnl-os.readthedocs.io/en/latest/getting_started.html)), and a 45-house Austin study found AC "energy slope" centered near 0.08 kW per degree C with audit characteristics correlating with higher slope ([MSU-hosted PDF](https://innovationcenter.msu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Development-and-analysis-of-residential-change-point-models-from-smart-meter-data.pdf), from a search summary only).

Disaggregation accuracy also depends on aggregation. A vendor evaluation using PG&E meter data found large estimation errors for almost all end uses, with accuracy best for end uses summed over a five-month period and worse for individual months or homes ([ACEEE 2016](https://www.aceee.org/files/proceedings/2016/data/papers/12_775.pdf)). One home's one summer is therefore weak evidence for a verdict, which argues for year-over-year comparison and requiring persistence.

The stakes of the faults themselves explain why a screening flag is still worth building, and also why it will be noisy. A summary of Proctor's work says 62% of more than 4,000 California residential systems tested were improperly charged (a secondary-source citation), a 10% supply-duct leak to outside costs about 10% of cooling capacity ([Proctor](https://www.proctoreng.com/dnld/92026A.pdf)), and a secondary source attributes roughly 17-23% extra energy to about 30% undercharge ([DOE](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2013/12/f5/issue3_airflow_charge.pdf)). ORNL simulation shows undercharge raises runtime and energy and that occupants may not notice, especially with oversized systems ([ORNL](https://www.ornl.gov/publication/impact-refrigerant-undercharge-faults-building-indoor-conditions-and-hvac-system)). Effect sizes of 10-25% sit in the same range as year-to-year weather and occupancy swings, so uncorrected comparisons will throw false positives. I found no published false-positive rate for any rule of this kind.

## Pool pumps are visible, but only some of the time

A pool pump is a multi-hour block at steady power. Hydro-Quebec's calculator assumes 1,390 W for a single-speed pump, 323 W low and 1,390 W high for two-speed, and 178 W low and 1,217 W high for variable-speed, for a Canadian unheated pool, so a proxy ([Hydro-Quebec](https://www.hydroquebec.com/residential/energy-wise/pools/efficient-pumps.html)). PNNL puts a 1.5 hp single-speed pump at about 2,000 W and says such pumps run at least 5-6 hours a day, if not around the clock ([PNNL](https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/variable-speed-pool-pumps)). In 15-minute kWh that is roughly 0.35-0.5 kWh per interval for hours on end; a variable-speed pump on low speed is about 0.05 kWh per interval, near the noise floor of refrigerator and standby loads (my arithmetic from those nameplate figures, not a published trace). California already bars sale or installation of new single-speed residential pumps of 1 hp or more under Title 20 ([Clean Energy Connection](https://cleanenergyconnection.org/service/variable-speed-pool-pumps)), so many candidates will be two-speed or variable-speed.

Academic detection exists. Burkhart et al. extracted filter-pump consumption from 15-minute data across 843 European households with **94.8% detection accuracy but 68.5% precision** using an off-the-shelf classifier ([FH Salzburg record](https://pure.fh-salzburg.ac.at/en/publications/detecting-swimming-pools-in-15-minute-load-data/), abstract only). A convolutional-network approach (PUMPNET) flags pump operation in half-hourly data across thousands of homes, though I could not retrieve its accuracy ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/s42162-020-00135-3)). Bidgely patents claim pool pump detection from 15-, 30- and 60-minute data via consistent multi-day start and stop times, and acknowledge AMI disaggregation cannot cover everything in a home ([USPTO 10630502](https://patents.justia.com/patent/10630502)); Bidgely's published ">90%" figure is for solar and EV detection, not pools ([Bidgely](https://go.bidgely.com/rs/492-VKN-623/images/EA-Grid-Planning.pdf)).

What is inferable follows from what a kWh stream contains. Defensible: pump running during SDG&E's 4-9 pm peak, continuous 24-hour running, run hours far above typical, and a step change in daily pump kWh at an unchanged schedule (a timer, speed or replacement change). Not supported by any source I found: motor wear, clogged filter, air in the system, leaks or impeller damage; a slow rise in per-interval kW at a fixed schedule is a hypothesis only, since filter pressure, temperature and heater use also move it. Savings claims for variable-speed retrofits diverge widely, from about 20% to 90% depending on the page: ENERGY STAR's 2014-era sheet claims about 70% less energy ([ENERGY STAR](https://www.energystar.gov/sites/default/files/asset/document/ES%20pool%20pump%20factsheet_080514_v5_0.pdf)), PNNL says 50-90% ([PNNL](https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/variable-speed-pool-pumps)), and current ENERGY STAR pages conflict with each other ([ENERGY STAR](https://www.energystar.gov/node/10446)). SDG&E's $200 variable-speed pump markdown expired 19 July 2021 ([SDG&E](https://www.sdge.com/sites/default/files/sdge-pool-pump-store-and-product-list_may_6_2021_final.pdf)); I could not confirm any current SDG&E or SDCP pool incentive, so we should not quote one. SDG&E's weekday 10 am-2 pm super off-peak window since 1 May 2026 reportedly suits pool pumps, but the source is a local news aggregator and the period should be confirmed on SDG&E's site ([Hoodline](https://hoodline.com/2026/04/sdg-e-daytime-power-plunge-gives-san-diego-a-midday-break/)).

## Solar masking decides which hours you can trust

For a NEM or net-billing home the meter sees net load. Behind-the-meter generation is invisible to the utility ([NREL](https://research-hub.nrel.gov/en/publications/estimation-of-behind-the-meter-solar-generation-by-integrating-ph-2/)), and PV degrades non-PV disaggregation especially in daytime ([Monash/C4NET](https://c4net.test.slickdesign.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/WP-1.2-PV-Disaggregation-and-Aggregation-Monash-University.pdf)). My reasoning, not a sourced ranking: dark-hours import (after sunset, before sunrise), the evening ramp, and the same hours in the same months under the same install state are valid; midday values, "export as production" (export is production minus simultaneous house load, as our wizard already notes) and summer-versus-spring daytime comparisons are not. Because pool pumps run mostly in daylight, their signal is largely masked on solar homes, which is why the pool flag should lean on peak-hour (4-9 pm) and overnight running, where solar does not hide it. It also means a failed inverter raises afternoon net import and looks like "more A/C use", so any A/C flag must be suppressed or caveated when the existing down-run or export-drop checks fire; missouri rate-case testimony makes the same point that regressions without a generation term can misread net-metered load, with peak-hour and daily regressions producing opposite-signed cooling coefficients ([Missouri PSC](https://efis.psc.mo.gov/Document/Display/805377), not peer-reviewed).

Reconstructing gross load or production from net data is a research problem. NREL-coauthored methods pair a physical PV model with a statistical load model and report 44% and 67% error reductions versus prior methods, relative figures with no absolute error given, and the better ones use cross-customer data a single-home tool lacks ([NREL](https://research-hub.nrel.gov/en/publications/estimation-of-behind-the-meter-solar-generation-by-integrating-ph-2/), [NREL](https://research-hub.nrel.gov/en/publications/joint-estimation-of-behind-the-meter-solar-generation-in-a-commun-2/)). Meter-only fault detection for solar is similarly weak: published AC-side methods use inverter power and call major underperformance below 60% of expected for 3 non-cloudy days and minor below 80% for 7 days ([pv magazine](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/02/20/how-to-detect-pv-system-underperformance-using-only-ac-side-inverter-data/)), and a meter cannot say which string failed. EV charging is the best-behaved confounder: hourly-data models reached about 75% detection at 35% false positives, tunable to 90% at 10% ([SINTEF](https://sintef.brage.unit.no/sintef-xmlui/handle/11250/2618624)), and 15-minute unsupervised methods exist ([Stony Brook](https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/behind-the-meter-disaggregation-of-residential-electric-vehicle-c/)), so an overnight step should be classified and removed before testing A/C.

Data handling is the unglamorous failure mode. SDG&E says its file holds up to 13 months of hourly or 15-minute usage in CSV or XML ([SDG&E](https://sdge.com/node/6181), via search summary). I could not verify whether a NEM file carries separate delivered and received channels or a signed net value, the column names, kWh versus Wh units, or SDG&E's daylight-saving handling. The ESPI XML format scales integer values by a power-of-ten multiplier ([Green Button Alliance](https://greenbuttonalliance.org/poweroftenmultiplier)), and interval files conventionally have 92 intervals on spring-forward days and 100 on fall-back ([ERCOT](https://www.ercot.com/files/docs/2015/12/08/interval_data_file_format_descriptions.doc)). The practical rules are to parse timezone-aware, count intervals per day, treat short days and daylight zeros as possible missing reads, and test the parser on a real SDG&E file before trusting any threshold.

## Rules we can defend, wording that survives scrutiny

No published HVAC-specific or pool-specific thresholds for 15-minute data exist, so the defensible position is to adopt rules whose logic is sound, label the numbers as ours, and tune them against outcomes. For A/C, the best-supported design is year-over-year: fit load against hourly outdoor temperature for the same months in both years, then flag a cooling-slope increase beyond the regression's 2-sigma residual band, requiring at least 3 months of data, a minimum count of hot days, and no change in the solar, EV or pool state between years (my design, unvalidated). A summer-versus-shoulder afternoon ratio is weaker, because weather differences dominate, and our current 2.5x ratio with 3+ spike days should be kept only as a "possible" tier. For pools, flag peak-hour running, near-continuous running and step changes in daily run hours, using the repeated-schedule logic from the Bidgely patent, and require several consecutive days plus same-season comparison because run hours rise in warm months. Given 68.5% precision in the one 15-minute pool study, a pool flag should always be a question to the homeowner ("do you have a pool pump on a timer?"), never an assertion.

Wording should follow evidence tiers tied to data volume. "Possible" fits under three months of data or a single anomaly; "likely" requires a pattern repeating across days or seasons plus a second source such as a production screenshot or bill; nothing is "confirmed" without a technician or manufacturer error code (my proposal). Sense's own help documentation states up front that it may not identify every device and improves with data ([Sense](https://help.sense.com/hc/en-us/articles/360001486688)), a model for stating limits, and users report frequent mislabeling by device-level disaggregation ([Green Building Advisor](https://www.greenbuildingadvisor.com/question/home-energy-monitors)). Vendor claims such as Oracle's "up to 99%" disaggregation accuracy are unaudited ([Oracle](https://docs.oracle.com/en/cloud/saas/readiness/utilities/2025/opower-oct25/25oct-util-opower-wn-f41253.htm)), and the commercial pattern is to sell these outputs as targeting and engagement insights rather than fault certifications: PSEG Long Island plans to use disaggregation to target pool-pump customers ([PSEG LI](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240307139429/en/5610495/PSEG-Long-Island-Expands-Partnership-with-Bidgely-to-Build-Comprehensive-Energy-Efficiency-Program)), with no validated accuracy reported. Suggested template: "Your meter data shows [pattern, dates], which is consistent with [cause]; it can also be caused by [benign cause]. We recommend a licensed [HVAC/pool] professional check it." Show the chart and dates, list at least one benign explanation, and give any dollar figure as an estimate with the rate and its date. Rate inputs need care: SDG&E's 4/1/26 EV-TOU-5 table shows super off-peak near $0.1285 summer ([SDG&E](https://www.sdge.com/sites/default/files/regulatory/4-1-26%20Schedule%20EV-TOU-5%20Total%20Rates%20Table.pdf)), while our code constant uses 6/1/26 values near $0.1226, so recheck which table applies before quoting.

## Licensing, referrals and data privacy set the action boundary

Acting on a flag is where legal risk concentrates. California treats referral fees as "illegal inducements" under BPC §7157(d), with civil penalties of $100-$1,000 for a first offense; referral services may maintain directories of licensed contractors but may not solicit or negotiate contracts for them; a license is required at $500 or more in labor and materials; unlicensed-activity citations run $200-$15,000 ([CSLB lead-generation bulletin](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/Media_Room/Industry_Bulletins/2020/January_9.aspx), via search snippet; [BPC 7028.7](https://california.public.law/codes/business_and_professions_code_section_7028.7)). CSLB's pool-construction C-53 scope does not mention pump replacement, and third-party sites assign pump repair to D-35, which I could not verify on CSLB's own page ([CSLB C-53](https://www2.cslb.ca.gov/about_us/library/licensing_classifications/Licensing_Classifications_Detail.aspx?Class=C53)). My reading, unverified: the safest path is a neutral "get an HVAC or pool evaluation" recommendation where the homeowner contracts directly with a licensed contractor, with no per-lead fee and no ad that implies we do the work, until counsel confirms what §7157(d) allows a non-licensee. I found no verified referral-fee economics for HVAC or pool partners, so none should be assumed. Cleaner commercial paths sit inside our own scope: EV rate-plan advice from published tariffs, battery and panel sizing, and NEM-expiry planning.

For data, CPUC D.11-07-056 sets privacy rules for utility-held usage data and third-party access, with D.12-08-045 extending it to gas and community choice customers ([CPUC](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/-/media/cpuc-website/divisions/energy-division/documents/demand-response/list-of-registered-demand-response-providers-drps_aggregators-and-faq/decision-d1107056.pdf), [CPUC docs](https://docs.cpuc.ca.gov/PublishedDocs/Published/G000/M026/K020/26020887.PDF)); Green Button Connect lets customers share data with authorized third parties ([NARUC](https://pubs.naruc.org/pub/AF1AF968-DB45-35FD-B452-CEB3663FA5C7)). When a homeowner uploads their own file, the utility-side rules largely drop out and, by my inference, CCPA/CPRA duties (notice at collection, purpose limitation, retention, deletion) and truthful-advertising law apply, subject to a revenue and volume threshold I did not verify for a firm our size ([Orrick](https://orrick.com/Insights/2020/05/WaitCCPA-20-What-Is-the-California-Privacy-Rights-Act-of-2020-and-Will-It-Become-Law), a 2020 pre-enactment piece). The 2025 CPPA rules on automated decision-making target significant decisions such as finance, housing and employment rather than marketing a service call, but sources conflict on effective dates ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2025/09/ccpa-2025-updated-regulations)). Pool detection also drew privacy concern from its own authors ([FH Salzburg](https://pure.fh-salzburg.ac.at/en/publications/detecting-swimming-pools-in-15-minute-load-data/)). Practical controls: use the file only for the evaluation the customer requested, store the summary rather than the raw file (as the wizard already does), disclose that, and delete on request. Suggested disclaimer elements for counsel review are that the analysis is an estimate from meter patterns that cannot diagnose equipment, that we are not HVAC or pool contractors, that any referral is optional and unpaid (only if true), and that rates change.

## What to build next in the portal

Our existing flags (solar export drops, idle stretches, heavy summer afternoon use, overnight EV-like steps) already form the right skeleton, and the research says to order them as gates. First, make the solar-health and EV checks run before the A/C check and suppress or downgrade the A/C card whenever a down-run, export drop or EV step is present, since each can mimic extra afternoon load. Second, replace the single summer-versus-shoulder ratio with a same-months year-over-year cooling-slope comparison against hourly outdoor temperature (needs a weather source and 13 months of data, which SDG&E's file can supply), keeping the ratio as a fallback "possible" tier. Third, add a pool-schedule card driven by dark-hours and peak-hour evidence: detect repeated multi-day blocks of roughly 0.3-0.5 kWh per 15 minutes, report run hours and any 4-9 pm overlap, and phrase it as a question, with an explicit "low-speed variable pumps are not detectable" note. Fourth, add an evidence-tier label (possible, likely) and a mandatory benign-explanation line to every card, and log each flag with its eventual outcome (did a contractor find a problem, did the customer have a pool) so thresholds can be tuned from our own data rather than guessed.

Before any of that ships, three verification tasks remain open and should block threshold tuning. Parse a real SDG&E NEM Green Button file to confirm import and export channels, units, header names, daylight-saving behavior and estimated-read markers, since none could be confirmed. Confirm the current SDG&E EV-TOU-5 rates and the 10 am-2 pm super off-peak period on SDG&E's own site. Have counsel confirm the referral-wording and disclaimer approach under BPC §7157(d) and the CCPA applicability thresholds. Read the unread primary sources (ORNL 2024 residential HVAC fault report, the Bidgely HVAC whitepaper, the Austin change-point paper, PUMPNET) if network access allows.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "can we diagnose" to "what can we honestly rank and ask." Interval data is a screening instrument whose reliability is capped by two things we cannot engineer away: 15-minute averaging, which erases the cycling signatures that define most A/C faults, and net metering, which hides the daytime hours when pools and air conditioners run hardest on solar homes. The strongest product is therefore not a fault detector but a well-gated triage that rules out solar and EV explanations first, reports only schedule and slope changes the data can carry, and turns each into a homeowner question or a neutral recommendation.

The novel implication is that our credibility depends more on what the tool declines to say than on what it flags: with 68.5% precision in the best pool study and effect sizes comparable to weather noise, the cost of one confident wrong call to a homeowner outweighs the value of several correct soft ones. Logging outcomes is what converts our unvalidated heuristics into the first defensible local thresholds, something no source in this research provides.
