# Reading solar health from Green Button data

Green Button interval data can tell a small SDG&E-area solar service company **when a system stopped exporting, roughly how much production it lost, and whether the export ceiling looks capped**. It cannot, on its own, tell you the inverter's size, the panel count, or whether a replacement inverter would add kilowatt-hours. The utility meter sees only the grid boundary, so gross PV production is never in the file; it must be inferred from export plus an unknown simultaneous household load. Everything below the level of "the system looks dead from date X" or "export flatlines at a constant level on clear days" is heuristic: the research found no peer-reviewed method that dates a residential PV fault from net meter data alone, and no accuracy figures for the clipping or sizing methods. Even the basic file contents are unverified for SDG&E, since no sample export was obtained: whether a residential account gets 15-minute or hourly intervals, and whether import and export appear as separate series, must be checked on a real file. The defensible product is therefore a screening report with stated ranges, backed by two cheap confirmations (the permit or interconnection documents and the inverter's own monitoring portal), not a verdict from the meter file alone.

## What the file contains and what it cannot show

SDG&E's Green Button page says the download covers **up to 13 months of smart-meter usage at hourly or 15-minute intervals**, available either by customer download ("Download My Data") or by authorizing a registered third-party app ("Connect My Data") ([SDG&E](https://sdge.com/node/6181)). Third-party guides disagree on resolution: one says residential accounts get hourly data and commercial get 15-minute, others say the customer picks at download ([Nectar](https://nectarclimate.com/utilities/san-diego-gas-electric-sdge); [Energy Toolbase](https://energytoolbase.com/newsroom/Blog/we-updated-our-green-button-data-instructions)). That conflict is unresolved, so the safe plan is to read the interval length from the file itself (900 versus 3,600 seconds in the standard XML format) and downgrade every conclusion in this report if the data is hourly. Hourly data blurs the 6 to 9 kW blocks of EV charging and understates 15-minute peak demand.

The standard XML format (ESPI) carries a reading type with units, a power-of-ten multiplier and a flow direction, and each interval reading has a unique start time ([Green Button Alliance](https://www.greenbuttonalliance.org/fb04); [multiplier](https://greenbuttonalliance.org/poweroftenmultiplier)). CSV layouts differ by utility, so a parser must auto-detect header rows, date format and interval length rather than hard-code SDG&E column names ([HelioScope](https://help-center.helioscope.com/hc/en-us/articles/8644752005523)). No SDG&E sample header was found, and SDG&E's own text describes "usage data" without mentioning export or generation. It is therefore **unconfirmed whether a net-metered home gets separate delivered and received columns or a single net value**. This matters more than any other file question: with a net-only series, a clean test of "was the system producing" becomes much harder, and with export-only data, midday self-consumption hides production completely.

The physics sets the second boundary. A bidirectional utility meter measures grid import and export; Export(t) = PV AC output minus simultaneous load (minus any battery charging). Gross production and midday load are never separately visible, so any peak export is only a lower bound on inverter output ([Eliq's disaggregation approach](https://support.eliq.com/knowledge/pv-disaggregation) and the academic literature all start from this problem; see also [Oxford, Abate](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/people/alessandro.abate/publications/BAR21.pdf)). Evening and overnight import is the reliable view of household load, because solar is off; midday load must be assumed. Units, timezone encoding, update latency and estimated-read flags in SDG&E files were not found. Timestamps in the XML format are UTC epoch and safe; local-time CSVs will have 92- and 100-interval days at the daylight-saving changes and an ambiguous repeated hour in November (standard practice, not sourced to SDG&E).

For access, the realistic route for a repair company is customer-initiated download: the homeowner logs into My Account, opens Usage, and uses the Green Button download below the usage graph, choosing date range, resolution and format ([Nectar guide](https://nectarclimate.com/utilities/san-diego-gas-electric-sdge)). Connect My Data requires a registered application reviewed by SDG&E, with a co-branding review and an indemnification clause ([SDG&E developer page](https://www.sdge.com/green-button/using-green-button-connect-my-data-developers)); no fee or timeline was found. It only makes sense if the company builds an ongoing monitoring product. CPUC privacy rules require customer authorization, limit disclosure to what is necessary for a stated purpose, and let customers revoke at any time ([CPUC](https://docs.cpuc.ca.gov/PublishedDocs/Published/G000/M077/K191/77191980.PDF)); whether they bind a company that merely receives a file from the homeowner was not established and needs counsel. Never collect a homeowner's SDG&E password.

## Judging inverter capacity from the export ceiling is a lower-bound exercise

The question "would a bigger inverter yield more kilowatt-hours" reduces to "is the array clipping." Clipping appears as a flat plateau at a nearly constant kilowatt level on clear days, and it is the economic loss from DC power exceeding inverter AC output. Industry sources put the loss at **under about 0.25 to 2 percent at a typical 1.2 to 1.3 DC-to-AC ratio, roughly 2 to 5 percent at 1.5, and above 9 percent at 1.7** ([Solar Power World](https://www.solarpowerworldonline.com/2016/07/solar-inverters-clipping-dcac-inverter-load-ratio-ideal/); [HelioScope](https://help-center.helioscope.com/hc/en-us/articles/8198321934867-Understanding-DC-AC-Ratio); [Folsom Labs](https://www.solarpowerworldonline.com/2017/09/folsom-rethinking-inverter-clipping/)). These are vendor and industry figures; no residential or San Diego clipping dataset from a national lab was found. The practical implication is that a typical correctly designed residential system gains little from a larger inverter, so a clipping claim needs evidence, not assumption.

The heuristic test, which is the researchers' engineering reasoning rather than a validated method, works as follows. Take clear days only (identified with a clear-sky model or the Reno and Hansen detection routine in [pvlib](https://pvlib-python.readthedocs.io/en/stable/reference/generated/pvlib.clearsky.detect_clearsky.html), whose thresholds were designed for one-minute data and need scaling for 15-minute data). Flag a plateau where export sits within roughly 1 to 2 percent of the day's maximum for at least three consecutive intervals while the clear-sky shape says output should be higher, and require the plateau level to repeat across days and seasons within about 3 to 5 percent (inverters derate with heat). If the daily maximum instead rises and falls with sun elevation, peaking in spring and fall for tilted modules, the inverter is not the limit. A plateau is only suggestive for three reasons. A **utility or interconnection export cap** produces an identical plateau independent of inverter rating, so the interconnection paperwork must be checked first. **Battery charging from PV** hides export and mimics a ceiling. And **microinverter systems** do not form one array-level plateau; the ceiling is the sum of per-unit AC limits, so a loss of one unit shows as a step of roughly 0.25 to 0.35 kW ([Enphase IQ8 datasheet](https://www.santansolar.com/wp-content/uploads/Enphase-IQ8-Microinverter-Datasheet.pdf)), and swapping a micro for a larger one is an entirely different decision from a string inverter replacement.

To convert a plateau into an inverter rating, add a base-load estimate to the 99th-percentile clear-day export; the result is "at least" that many kilowatts, with uncertainty equal to the base-load error, which the notes assume is 0.3 to 1.0 kW for a San Diego home (an unsourced assumption). To estimate the energy actually lost, reconstruct the unclipped bell from the non-clipped shoulders and integrate the shortfall over the plateau intervals at 0.25 hours each. That reconstruction is the weakest link: it requires a model of tilt and azimuth, and it inherits any load that was running at noon.

Array size and module wattage are harder still, and the report should say so plainly. DC kilowatts can be estimated as the best equinox-period clear-day (export plus base load) divided by the modeled clear-sky output per kilowatt, using pvlib and a PVWatts-style loss of about 14 percent ([pvlib](https://pvlib-python.readthedocs.io/en/latest/reference/generated/pvlib.modelchain.ModelChain.with_pvwatts.html)). When a plateau exists, use the shoulder hours instead of the peak. The heuristic error is **roughly 15 to 20 percent** (the notes' own estimate, combining base load, soiling and temperature, tilt and azimuth mismatch, and degradation; no published accuracy exists for this method). Annual cross-check: San Diego specific yield is around 1,600 to 1,700 kilowatt-hours per kilowatt-year by third-party calculators (about 1,660 per [Voltflow](https://www.voltflow.net/solar-panel-calculator/city/san-diego)), but gross annual production is not observable from the meter, so the check needs inverter-portal data. Panel count then equals DC kilowatts divided by module wattage, and 20 modules of 400 W equals 16 of 500 W, so the meter data cannot resolve count versus wattage; the permit set, a Google Solar roof count, or the system's installation year (2013 SunPower modules were around 327 W per the notes) must settle it. In short, the interval data can say the ceiling is consistent with, or contradicts, the stated equipment; it cannot establish the equipment.

For the replacement decision, the arithmetic is more reliable than the detection. A DC-to-AC ratio is array kilowatts over old AC kilowatts. A 7.6 kW inverter at a 1.3 ratio implies about 9.9 kW of panels, and replacing it with a Powerwall 3, whose integrated solar inverter is rated **11.5 kW continuous AC with 20 kW maximum solar input across six MPPTs** ([Tesla manual](https://service.tesla.com/docs/Public/Energy/Powerwall/Powerwall-3-with-Gateway-3-Installation-Manual-NA-EN/GUID-EC527BC7-4750-4425-BBC4-DB8C000339B3.html)), would remove clipping on any ordinary residential array. The recovered energy is bounded by the clipped energy actually measured, which for a normally sized system is a few percent of annual production. The honest answer to "will a new inverter produce more" is therefore: only if the plateau analysis shows repeated, season-stable clipping, or if the existing inverter is partially failed, and the second case is the one that usually justifies a swap.

## Dating faults and restarts: a daily-ratio changepoint with a 15-minute scan

A dead inverter shows as export near zero and midday net load resembling the overnight base load, on a day when a clear-sky model says strong generation was available. The discriminator against cloud is an external irradiance reference: the NREL National Solar Radiation Database gives 30-minute irradiance at roughly 4 km resolution, validated to about 5 percent bias for global horizontal irradiance ([NREL](https://nrel.gov/docs/fy22osti/70627.pdf)), which must be interpolated to 15 minutes and which cannot resolve a single roof's cloud edge. A cloudy day still shows a noisy, positive, irradiance-correlated reduction in net load; a dead system shows essentially none. A **grid outage** is a different signature: exact zero import and zero export for a run of intervals in the middle of the day, because the meter sees no energy at all. A vacation does not explain a missing midday export on a clear day, because low load only makes export larger. No peer-reviewed paper was found that detects a residential PV outage from net metering data alone; the adjacent work covers behind-the-meter disaggregation at hourly resolution ([Bu et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.00734)) and utility-side outage localization ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10394249)), and accuracy at 15 minutes is unknown. SDG&E reliability reports are aggregate and cannot date one home's outage ([CPUC](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/-/media/cpuc-website/divisions/energy-division/documents/infrastructure/electric-reliability-reports/2020-sdge-electric-reliability-report.pdf)).

The recommended pipeline, all heuristic as a composition though each piece is established, builds a daily scalar such as 10:00 to 15:00 export energy divided by clear-sky expected energy, or midday net load minus night baseline, using median smoothing so step edges survive. Run a changepoint method such as PELT from the `ruptures` library with a minimum segment of three to five days on the clear-day subset ([ruptures](https://ctruong.perso.math.cnrs.fr/ruptures-docs/build/html/_modules/ruptures/detection/pelt.html)), with the penalty tuned so a sustained drop of about 40 percent yields one break and cloud noise yields none. Then scan the 15-minute data on the transition day: the last interval tracking the clear-sky curve and the first interval flatlining bound the fault time to within one interval. Restart is the same method on the recovery step, confirmed when clear-day performance returns to within roughly 10 to 15 percent of its prior level. Report an interval, not a point: if the days before the first zero were cloudy, the true start is earlier than the first observed missing export, and a trip overnight cannot be placed within the day at all.

Partial failures are detectable only when large. A lost string or MPPT lowers production roughly in proportion to the capacity lost, so one of two equal MPPTs down looks like a clear-day peak and daily energy near half of the system's own earlier level, with an otherwise normal bell shape; one of 20 microinverters is about 5 percent, which is below the practical floor. The notes put that **detection floor at losses of about 10 to 15 percent of production** (heuristic; no source quantifies it). Distinguish fault from aging: median field degradation is about **0.5 percent per year** ([NREL](https://www.nrel.gov/pv/lifetime.html)), and a 2023 study of 834 modules found a median near 0.58 percent per year ([NREL](https://nrel.gov/docs/fy23osti/82352.pdf)), so a step of more than about 5 percent within days is a fault rather than wear, while year-over-year changes below roughly 5 to 10 percent are not distinguishable from irradiance bias in a single year. Shading appears as a dip at the same clock times each clear day drifting slowly with the sun path, and soiling as a gradual decline recovering after rain; both are qualitative here, with no San Diego magnitudes sourced. Normalize to the shape of daily integrals rather than raw peak export, because the peak depends on instantaneous load.

Intermittent faults show up at coarse resolution only as patterns. Isolation (ground) faults typically occur at morning start-up from moisture and clear as the array dries; SolarEdge-type inverters test DC-to-ground resistance at every power-on and error below about 600 kilo-ohms ([installer training page](https://sites.google.com/ionsolar.com/field-support/trainings/monitoring-platforms/solaredge/troubleshooting/isolation-faults), not a manufacturer document). In interval data that is a repeated late-morning start. Reconnection after a grid event is typically 300 seconds under IEEE 1547 settings, though a Tesla Powerwall profile lists 15 seconds for the California Rule 21 2022 grid code ([Schneider](https://www.se.com/us/en/faqs/FA308830); [Tesla](https://service.tesla.com/docs/Public/Energy/Powerwall/Device-Setup-in-Tesla-Pros-NA-EN/GUID-CE784C0E-5D39-43A6-B196-D64FBC9090A2.html)). Either way a restart delay is smaller than one interval, so the data shows at most one partially reduced interval and **no restart time can be stated more precisely than plus or minus 15 minutes**. If import also dips around a notch, suspect a grid event; if load keeps being served, suspect the inverter. Error-code identities such as the SolarEdge isolation code were not confirmed against manufacturer manuals.

Lost dollars depend on the tariff, which the interval file does not tell you. Under NEM 1.0 and 2.0, exports are credited at retail rates; under Net Billing Tariff, exports are credited at avoided-cost values, usually well below retail, and net surplus at true-up is paid at wholesale-type rates in all cases ([CPUC](https://webtraining.cpuc.ca.gov/industries-and-topics/electrical-energy/demand-side-management/customer-generation/net-energy-metering-and-net-billing)). A vendor source claims about 3.7 cents per kilowatt-hour average SDG&E export credit ([Stellar Solar](https://stellarsolar.net/2026/03/02/sdge-net-metering-in-2026-rate-structures-explained/)), which is unverified. The right calculation re-runs the hourly bill with the lost kilowatt-hours added back, not a flat multiplier, and it must be shown as a range. Lost kilowatt-hours themselves come from three baselines (the system's own earlier clear-day yield scaled by irradiance, the prior year's same period, and a modeled value from permit size), and the spread between them is the honest error band.

## Other analyses the data supports, ranked by reliability

Evening battery sizing is the strongest add-on, because evening import is a true load measurement. Compute average and 80th-percentile 4 to 9 pm import by season and midday export, and size on both energy and power: Powerwall 3 provides 13.5 kWh and 11.5 kW continuous discharge, with a 5 kW AC charge limit ([Tesla datasheet](https://digitalassets.tesla.com/tesla-contents/image/upload/powerwall-3-datasheet-na-en.pdf)). The notes' sizing rule, which is a heuristic, is units for energy equal to the 80th-percentile evening import divided by about 12 usable kWh, and units for power equal to peak evening import over 11.5 kW. Value depends on the tariff regime: under NEM 1.0 and 2.0 the battery mostly provides backup and virtual power plant revenue because exports already earn near retail, while under NBT the shifted kilowatt-hours earn the gap between on-peak import cost and the low export credit. Round-trip efficiency near 89 to 90 percent is a third-party figure ([Clean Energy Reviews](https://www.cleanenergyreviews.info/blog/tesla-powerwall-3-review)). Rates must be checked against SDG&E's own rate tables: two public sources conflict on summer on-peak (about 70 cents versus 56 cents), so any figure shown to a customer should be labeled illustrative ([Stellar Solar](https://stellarsolar.net/2026/03/02/sdge-tou-rates-in-2026-everything-you-need-to-know/)). The San Diego Community Power battery program has an upfront payment, performance payments for discharge in a fixed window, and a five-year enrollment, per third-party and Tesla pages that conflict on amounts ([Tesla](https://www.tesla.com/support/energy/virtual-power-plant/sdcp)).

True-up modeling from net data is feasible but bounded. Net annual import minus export and a monthly running dollar balance are directly computable; gross consumption is inferred as import plus modeled production minus export, with the model's error of roughly 10 to 15 percent (unsourced) carried through. Fewer than 12 months of data makes any annual figure an extrapolation, so weather-normalize or flag it. The tariff generation (NEM 1.0, 2.0 or NBT) must come from the customer's bill or interconnection date, never be inferred from data; NEM 2.0 customers keep their tariff for 20 years from interconnection ([Stellar Solar](https://stellarsolar.net/2026/03/02/sdge-net-metering-in-2026-rate-structures-explained/)).

Load-shape analyses are all heuristic and work best in solar-off hours: a 10th-percentile overnight baseload times 8,760 hours and the rate gives a standby-waste estimate, with the 0.6 kW flag threshold being a rule of thumb; temperature regression of evening import (CalTRACK-style, see [Recurve](https://recurve.com/how-it-works/caltrack-hourly-methods)) gives a cooling slope; repeating 6 to 11 kW rectangles lasting 2 to 6 hours at night suggest a Level 2 EV; a fixed-time daily 1 to 2 kW block suggests a pool pump that could shift to super-off-peak. Net metering masks midday air conditioning, so these should be presented as "likely," not as findings. Electrification headroom compares annual surplus export to estimated EV and heat-pump loads; under NBT, surplus is worth little as export, so using it on-site is the pitch.

Panel headroom is the weakest use. A 200 A service is 48 kW of nameplate. The notes cite NEC 220.87, which allows documented 12-month peak demand plus 25 percent to prove existing load, as the reason interval data matters, but they flag it as general knowledge not verified against the current edition or local rules. The 15-minute average understates instantaneous peaks and solar masks true midday load, and the 120 percent busbar rule needs a photo of the main panel that the meter cannot supply. The report should only ever say "likely no panel upgrade needed, pending site check." Browser JavaScript suffices for parsing, quality checks, rate bucketing, battery sizing, true-up and event detection, keeping customer data on the client; clear-sky modeling, irradiance fetches and changepoint detection fit a server-side function, with pvlib and ruptures being Python-only and PVWatts API behavior unverified.

## Conclusion

The central insight is that the meter file is better at **time** than at **magnitude**. It is reasonably good at saying when export stopped and restarted and whether a ceiling repeats across seasons, but it cannot establish inverter size, array size or module wattage, and every sizing number inherits a hidden load term of unknown size. That argues for treating Green Button as the first, cheap screen in a two-source workflow: the interval file proves a problem exists and dates it, while the inverter portal and permit documents supply the equipment facts the file cannot. The largest unverified assumptions are that SDG&E residential files carry 15-minute data and separate import and export channels; the first customer file collected should be used to settle both before any tool is built.

A second implication is commercial: for ordinary systems the clipping upside of a bigger inverter is small, so the strongest defensible case for an inverter or Powerwall 3 swap is a measured failure, not a measured ceiling.

## Recommended workflow for a small company

**Ask the homeowner for** four things before any analysis. First, a Green Button download covering the full 13 months, at 15-minute resolution if offered, in XML (ESPI) format if possible with CSV as a fallback; have them download it themselves or screen-share rather than ever sharing a password. Second, their most recent SDG&E bill, to establish the rate plan and the net-metering generation (NEM 1.0, 2.0 or NBT). Third, the inverter model and, if they have it, a screenshot or export from the inverter monitoring portal (SolarEdge, Enphase Enlighten, SMA or similar), which supplies true production and settles the load-masking problem. Fourth, the interconnection or permit paperwork and photos of the inverter nameplate and displayed error codes, which give the AC rating, any export cap, module count and model. Get written consent that names the data, the purpose, retention, no resale and the right to revoke, and have counsel confirm the language.

**Compute, in this order.** Run data quality checks first: interval length, expected 96 intervals per day, daylight-saving days, gaps, duplicates, negative values, units, and coverage under about 90 percent of a month (downgrade or refuse). Then compute the cheap, reliable items: evening 4 to 9 pm import and midday export by season, overnight baseload, and monthly net balance. Next, with a clear-sky model and irradiance data, build the clear-day mask, the daily normalized-yield series and the changepoint dates, then scan the 15-minute data at each break. Only then test for a clipping plateau and, if one repeats across seasons, estimate lost energy. Treat any DC size or module count as a plausibility check against the permit, never a finding. Where a monitoring export exists, use it as the primary production source and the meter file for import and export only.

**Tell the customer** what the data shows with its interval attached: "export stopped between these dates, an estimated X to Y kilowatt-hours of production and roughly $A to $B were lost under your tariff, here is what we assumed," a plain average-day chart with the 4 to 9 pm band shaded, and a clear next step, which is an on-site inspection to read error codes and test strings. Present battery and load findings as estimates with ranges, labeled with the data period.

**Do not claim** that the interval data identifies which string, microinverter or component failed; a restart or fault time finer than 15 minutes; an inverter, array or module specification derived from the meter; that a larger inverter will add a stated number of kilowatt-hours without a measured plateau, a checked export cap and the permit data; a precise dollar savings figure from fewer than 12 months of data or from unverified rates; a panel-upgrade pass or fail; or any statement that a lost-production period is covered by a warranty, which depends on terms and cause the file cannot show.
