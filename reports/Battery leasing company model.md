# Own the Powerwall Lease, Fund It Prepaid

Solar Review Corp can build its own small battery leasing company, and the structure to copy is not a true "$0 lease" but Participate's actual product: a **prepaid lease with $0 monthly payments**, where the homeowner pays one discounted amount (about 70% of the system price) up front, Solar Review (or a lessor company it controls) keeps title for at least six years, claims the **30% §48E investment tax credit** that leased batteries still earn after the 2025 tax law, sells that credit for cash under §6418, and offers a fair-market-value buyout from year 6. The tax credit survives for storage through construction starts in 2033, but it is all-or-nothing on the new foreign-sourcing ("FEOC") test, and nobody has yet shown a Tesla certification that Powerwall 3 passes it. The numbers are thinner than the headline 30% suggests: because Solar Review would own its own sales channel, its credit is figured on what it actually pays Cosmic (~$12,500), not on the $15,000 retail price, so offering the same 30% customer discount Participate offers leaves roughly **$3,000 of cash out the door per system on day one**, recovered by the credit sale months later. Whether owning the lease beats selling through Participate therefore depends on three items still unconfirmed: whether the SDCP battery incentive ($3,375 standard, $4,725 CARE) can be paid to the lessor, what a six-year-old Powerwall is worth at buyout, and whether Powerwall 3 passes FEOC. On Solar Review's own figures, a 25-system San Diego pilot roughly matches the ~$37,500 company margin Participate deals pay today if SDCP flows to the lessor, and loses about $40,000 if it does not; the real prize is removing a counterparty that is already late paying Cosmic, plus the residual value and grid-service income Participate currently keeps. The first gates are legal (a California contractor license or an installer-contracts structure, a Utah retailer registration and bond, a lease that is a true lease), then tax (an S-corporation lessor rule that may block the credit, and FEOC proof), and only then capital, which at pilot scale is about $75,000–$100,000 of working capital.

**How to read this report.** Every outside source behind this report was read as a **search-result summary only**: the research sandbox blocked full-page fetches of IRS, Treasury, state code, law-firm and vendor pages, so no statute, regulation or contract other than the Participate agreement was read in full. The Participate Energy Customer Agreement (California, 5-day, Installation v1.0, 2026-06-01, pre-filled for Cosmic) was the one document read cover to cover, and it is cited by PDF page. Figures labeled "Solar Review's own figures" are the internal reference numbers supplied for this analysis, not market data. Arithmetic is mine and every assumption is shown. Items marked **[uncertain]** must be confirmed before money moves. This is a business model and knowledge base, **not legal or tax advice**.

## Participate's "$0 lease" is really a prepaid lease, and only that version works

Participate's own disclosure page states the product plainly: "This is a Prepaid Lease. You will make progress payments totaling the Net Amount Due during the installation phase. Monthly Lease Payments: $0.00" ([Participate Agreement p.8](file:///root/.claude/uploads/770fd2b9-36aa-5fea-be24-88904b143f5e/42bc33f7-CA_-_5_Day_Participate_Energy_-_Customer_Agreement__Installation_v.1.0_-_20260601.pdf)). The customer pays a **Baseline Lease Amount minus "Included Incentives" received by the System Owner**, in two progress payments made to the installer acting as Participate's collection agent, with the first payment capped at the lesser of $1,000 or 10% (pp.9, 13, 29). Cosmic's installation contract price is literally **$0.00** (p.38), which keeps California's down-payment rules intact while making Participate the only party the customer owes. The battery term is **10 years** (PV is 25), purchase is allowed only **on the sixth anniversary and each anniversary after**, or after year 6 on a home sale, at **fair market value minus remaining obligations owed to the customer, floor $0**, with an appraiser only if the parties disagree (pp.12, 21). Participate keeps every tax credit, depreciation deduction, rebate and grid-service right unless Exhibit C says otherwise, and takes **70% of virtual-power-plant proceeds** on programs it "developed" (30% on others, its call), rising to 100% after the battery term (pp.14, 19). Consumer guides put the discount at roughly 30% ([solar.com](https://www.solar.com/learn/participate-energys-prepaid-lease-a-consumer-guide/)), and an installer release describes the one-time payment as 70% of lease value ([Power Solutions](https://www.einpresswire.com/article/916587033/power-solutions-offering-long-island-solar-installations-through-the-participate-energy-program)). Maxwell Power, the San Diego platform rebranded from HDM Renewable Finance in June 2026 and backed by reported Fairtide commitments of $750M to $1.8B (figures conflict), sells the same idea as a prepaid PPA for Powerwall-only, solar plus Powerwall, or solar ([pv magazine USA](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/06/02/hdm-renewable-finance-rebrands-as-maxwell-power-raises-additional-funding/); [Renewables Now](https://renewablesnow.com/news/maxwell-power-secures-usd-800m-from-for-consumer-solar-storage-1302263/)).

A lease where the homeowner pays **nothing at all** does not work, for money reasons and tax reasons. With no rent, the lessor recovers ~$12,500 only through a ~$3,000 net credit, an incentive it may not be allowed to collect, a few hundred dollars a year of grid payments, and a residual. On the tax side, a no-rent lease has weak profit-motive evidence, and if the old noncorporate-lessor rule still governs an S-corporation lessor, its test that business deductions exceed **15% of rental income** in the first 12 months is impossible with zero rental income ([Charlson opinion excerpt](https://law.onecle.com/tax/2001/charlsonopn.tcm.wpd04.html); [Schiff v. U.S.](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/942/348/282390/)). Whether that rule (former §46(e)(3)) limits §48E credits today was **not confirmed either way** and is the single most important question for tax counsel; a C-corporation or partnership lessor may avoid it. The marketing message can stay "$0 a month," but the contract must be a prepaid lease.

Three features keep it a true lease, with the lessor as tax owner. First, the purchase option must float at genuine fair market value: Rev. Proc. 2001-28 is summarized as disallowing fixed-price lessee options and accepting FMV options ([Akin Gump, Burton slides](https://akingump.com/images/content/3/5/v2/35209/Sale-Leasbacks-Feb-2015-D-Burton.pdf)), and California treats a lease that hands over ownership for nominal consideration as a security agreement as a matter of law ([Mondaq on California Finance Lenders Law](https://webiis08.mondaq.com/unitedstates/antitrust-eu-competition/11048/california-finance-lenders-law)). A nominal buyout would turn the deal into a disguised credit sale, move the credit to the homeowner (who can no longer claim §25D), and pull in Truth in Lending and possibly a California Financing Law license. Second, no title transfer before the five-year recapture window closes. Third, the lessor must keep real residual risk. Here the prepaid design has a soft spot worth naming: Rev. Proc. 2001-28 asks that the lessor's unconditional investment stay at least **20% of cost throughout the term** ([Tax Notes text](https://taxnotes.com/lr/resolve//dph6)), and a lessor that collects 70% from the customer and ~25% from a credit sale has recovered almost everything in year one. That procedure is an advance-ruling safe harbor, not the law, and courts weigh substance under Frank Lyon-style factors; the industry's ~70% prepay plus year-6 FMV option is the recognized pattern ([solar.com](https://www.solar.com/learn/prepaid-solar-leases-ppas/); [Solarinsure](https://www.solarinsure.com/buyers-guide-prepaid-solar-5-common-questions)). Counsel should still sign off on the exact prepay percentage.

Two changes to the requested design follow. **Make the term 10 years with a buyout option opening at year 6, not a 6-year term that ends in a buyout.** A lease that expires at year 6 with "buy it or we remove it" makes purchase reasonably certain, which weakens true-lease status; Participate's 10-year battery term with an optional year-6 window avoids that. And **define the FMV method in the contract** (independent appraisal, who picks and pays the appraiser, the income-and-cost approach used) while telling customers honestly that the buyout is not guaranteed to be near zero; Participate's own contract leaves the method undefined (p.21), which is the source of the "year-6 buyout surprise" consumer complaints visible in forum anecdotes ([SolarPanelTalk](https://www.solarpaneltalk.com/forum/solar-panels-for-home/solar-panels-for-your-home/421313-ppa-end-of-year-5-buyout-option-worth-it-or-prepay-or-keep-the-lease)).

## The 30% credit survives for batteries, but FEOC decides whether it exists

**Storage kept its full §48E schedule.** The 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) put wind and solar on an accelerated cliff (placed in service after 2027 unless construction began by July 4, 2026) but that amendment "does not affect related energy storage technology" ([Eide Bailly](https://www.eidebailly.com/insights/alerts/2025/obbb-energy-credits); [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/08/irs-releases-updated-obbba-related-energy-credit-guidance)). Batteries keep the full credit for construction beginning through 2033, then 75% in 2034, 50% in 2035 and zero from 2036 ([Fallbrook](https://fallbrookfinancialservices.com/battery-storage-tax-credits-obbba/); [ICS Tax](https://ics-tax.com/news/48e-investment-tax-credit-obba-phaseout/)); one vendor says "through 2032," so confirm against §48E(e) **[uncertain]**. OBBBA's new leased-residential bar, §48E(i), reaches only solar water heating and small wind, not batteries ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/focus-on-clean-energy-tax-provisions-in-the-one-big-beautiful-bill-act/); [Kirkland](https://www.kirkland.com/publications/kirkland-alert/2025/08/one-big-beautiful-bill-act-brings-big-changes-to-green-energy-tax-credits)). Meanwhile §25D ended for homeowner expenditures after December 31, 2025, so **a business owner leasing the battery is now the only federal-credit path** for a California or Utah homeowner, which is why third-party ownership is projected to jump from roughly 45% of residential installs in 2025 to 60–69% in 2026 (secondary projections) ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/focus-on-clean-energy-tax-provisions-in-the-one-big-beautiful-bill-act/); [GoSolo](https://gosolo.io/blog/2025-solar-market-outlook-post-25d/)).

**Each home gets 30%, not 6%, without wage paperwork.** The §48E base rate is 6%, multiplied by five when prevailing wage and apprenticeship rules are met or the property is under 1 MW. For storage the One Megawatt Exception uses the device's maximum net output, and the final regulations aggregate storage only when it shares an owner, a placed-in-service year **and an interconnection point** ([26 CFR §1.48E-3](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.48E-3); [Ever.green summary of final regs](https://ever.green/insight/45y-and-48e-tax-credit-final-rules)). A ~11.5 kW Powerwall 3 behind its own meter is its own sub-1 MW property, so **30%** applies with no prevailing-wage records; confirm the exact "same interconnection point" text **[uncertain]**.

**The bonus adders are mostly not bankable yet.** Domestic content adds 10 points but requires 50% U.S. manufactured-product content for 2026 construction starts and 55% after 2026 ([Reunion](https://www.reunioninfra.com/insights/domestic-content-bonus-credit-requirements-thresholds-and-safe-harbor); [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2025/07/from-ira-to-obbba-a-new-era-for-clean-energy-tax-credits)), with a battery safe-harbor table in Notice 2025-08 ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-25-08.pdf)). Powerwall 3 is assembled in Lathrop, California with cells partly sourced from partners ([NuWatt](https://nuwattenergy.com/en/battery-storage/products/tesla-powerwall-3)), and a distributor lists a "Domestic Content Model" for which no Tesla document was found ([CSE Solar](https://csesolarusa.com/product/tesla-powerwall-3/)); do not price this adder without a signed, batch-specific certification. The energy-community adder (+10) is address-specific: Notice 2026-39 refreshed the statistical-area and coal-closure lists in June 2026, and addresses can be checked on the federal map ([IRS Notice 2026-39](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-26-39.pdf); [Novogradac](https://www.novoco.com/notes-from-novogradac/irs-updates-energy-community-bonus-credit-eligibility-novogradac-policy-map-updated-to-reflect-2026-changes)); few coastal San Diego tracts are likely to qualify, Utah coal-closure tracts might. The **low-income §48E(h) bonus is effectively unavailable**: it is a competitive annual allocation, and commentary on the proposed rules says standalone storage is not a "qualified facility" for it ([Legal500](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/united-states/energy-and-natural-resources/proposed-regulations-regarding-section-48e-clean-electricity-low-income-communities-bonus-credit-program); [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/node/125331)).

**FEOC is the deadline that actually matters.** For storage beginning construction in 2026, the material-assistance cost ratio (non-prohibited-foreign-entity share of manufactured-product cost) must be at least **55%**, rising about five points a year to 75%, and failing it **disallows the entire credit** ([Alvarez & Marsal](https://www.alvarezandmarsal.com/thought-leadership/irs-issues-notice-2026-15-first-guidance-on-prohibited-foreign-entity-rules-for-energy-tax-credits); [Nixon Peabody on Notice 2026-15](https://www.nixonpeabody.com/insights/alerts/2026/03/20/foreign-entities-of-concern-the-material-assistance-rule-and-irs-notice-2026-15)). Tesla's U.S. storage cells were largely sourced from Chinese suppliers ([Energy-Storage.News](https://www.energy-storage.news/teslas-energy-division-gross-margin-declines-19-despite-battery-storage-deployment-rebound/)), and one estimate puts a battery with only PFE cells at ~48%, below the line ([China Prospect](https://chinaprospect.com/battery-storage-tax-credit-foreign-entity-rules/)). Installer pages calling Powerwall 3 "FEOC compliant" cite no Tesla source ([NuWatt matrix](https://nuwattenergy.com/en/domestic-content-feoc-equipment-matrix-2026)). **No Powerwall should be leased on a credit-dependent price until Tesla or the distributor provides a written material-assistance certification for that batch**, and the threshold must be rechecked every year as it rises.

**Basis is cost, and vertical integration shrinks it.** The credit base is the lessor's actual cost of the property; related-party values get "more closely scrutinized," and Treasury's Inspector General probed SolarCity over inflated fair-market-value claims ([Treasury basis guidance](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/216/N-Evaluating_Cost_Basis_for_Solar_PV_Properties-final.pdf); [ILSR](https://archive.ilsr.org/treasury-dept-fingers-solarcity-leasing/)). Participate buys the system from an unrelated dealer at a retail-like price, so its 30% applies to something near the full system price. Solar Review owning its own leases would figure 30% on roughly the **$12,500 it pays Cosmic**, plus whatever soft costs a CPA lets it capitalize (whether rep commissions belong in basis is unconfirmed). Routing systems through a separate entity at a marked-up "sale" price to inflate basis is exactly the related-party step-up the IRS challenges; it needs an independent appraisal and is not recommended at pilot scale ([Akin Gump](https://akingump.com/images/content/3/5/v2/35209/Sale-Leasbacks-Feb-2015-D-Burton.pdf)).

**Recapture sets the buyout date.** If the battery is sold or stops qualifying within five years of being placed in service, the credit is recaptured at 100%, 80%, 60%, 40% and 20% in years one through five, then zero ([NuWatt](https://nuwattenergy.com/en/solar-lease-ppa-2026/section-48-homeowner-guide)). A buyer of the credit stays liable for recapture, which is why buyers demand indemnities or insurance ([CFO Dive](https://www.cfodive.com/spons/structure-matters-how-itc-transfers-mitigate-risk-and-maintain-compliance/699715/)), and casualty losses can trigger it unless the property is restored ([Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.projectfinance.law/tax-equity-news/2021/june/itc-recapture-following-a-casualty-event/)). Participate's contract mirrors this exactly: purchase only from year 6, lender termination and capital-provider repossession only after the fifth anniversary (pp.17, 21–22). A home sale should be handled by **assigning the lease to the buyer**, not by an early buyout.

**Credit transfer is the only realistic monetization, and small batches price low.** §6418 lets a taxpayer sell the credit once for cash, after registering each facility in IRS Energy Credits Online; an unregistered facility's election is ineffective ([26 USC §6418](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6418); [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/node/128071)). Small solar deals averaged about **86¢ rising to 91¢** per credit dollar in 2024, with pricing roughly flat into Q1 2026 and the residential segment "struggling" ([Crux 1Q2026](https://www.cruxclimate.com/blog/1q2026-market-update-qa-josh-price)). A vendor guide puts the practical floor near **$250,000 of credits** with 70–90¢ pricing **[uncertain]** ([Veckta](https://veckta.com/selling-tax-credits/)), and the smallest public example is a $600,000 credit sale estimated at 85–87¢ ([pv magazine USA](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2024/06/25/solar-tax-transfer-for-smaller-projects-dissecting-a-600000-tax-credit-transaction)). Buyers often require tax-credit insurance at about **2–3% one-time premium**, usually paid by the seller ([Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.projectfinance.law/tax-equity-news/tax-credit-insurance-mitigates-risk-for-tax-credit-transfers-but-due-diligence-is-still-necessary/)), and may not pay until as late as March 15 of the following year ([Crux on bridge loans](https://www.cruxclimate.com/blog/tax-credit-bridge-loans-in-clean-energy-capital-markets)). A 25-system pilot generates under $100,000 of credits, so plan for one pooled annual sale through a marketplace such as Basis Climate or Crux, at **about 80¢ net** after costs in this model. Tax equity, with historical minimums of $25–50M, is out of reach ([Solar Power World](https://www.solarpowerworldonline.com/2024/12/tax-transferability-a-win-for-the-little-guys-in-solar/)).

**Depreciation mostly shelters the prepaid rent.** OBBBA permanently restored **100% bonus depreciation** for property acquired after January 19, 2025, on a basis reduced by half the credit ([Accounting Today](https://www.accountingtoday.com/opinion/what-cpas-should-know-about-the-return-of-100-bonus-depreciation-in-2025); [Etica](https://eticaag.com/maximizing-tax-benefits-macrs-bonus-depreciation-bess/)); whether §48E storage stays in the 5-year MACRS class is disputed among sources but largely moot under 100% bonus ([Louis Plung](https://www.louisplung.com/obbba-changes-to-energy-credits/); [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/focus-on-clean-energy-tax-provisions-in-the-one-big-beautiful-bill-act/)). On a $12,500 system with a $3,750 credit, the depreciable basis is **$10,625**, which happens to roughly offset a $10,500 prepayment if that prepayment is taxed as rent when received (general tax background, not sourced here; §467 timing must be confirmed). Because equipment leasing is generally a passive rental activity at the shareholder level, depreciation losses and retained credits can be trapped against Dennis's non-passive commission income ([The Tax Adviser](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2023/apr/interaction-of-s-shareholders-loss-limitations/)); selling the credit under §6418 sidesteps that for the credit. Financing with nonrecourse debt can shrink the credit base under §49 ([uscode §49](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-121/t26/s49/)), and California does not follow federal bonus depreciation (general knowledge, CPA to confirm).

## One Powerwall earns about what Participate pays, unless SDCP and the residual come home

The lessor's income is wider than the credit. In order of reliability it is: the customer prepayment (rent); the §48E credit sold for cash; the depreciation shield; the **SDCP Solar Battery Savings** upfront incentive (~$250/kWh when adding a battery to existing solar, $350/kWh for CARE/FERA, i.e. **$3,375 / $4,725** per 13.5 kWh Powerwall, matching Solar Review's own figures) plus **$0.10/kWh for weekday-evening discharge** with a 90% monthly discharge requirement and partial clawback if un-enrolled within five years ([SolarQuarter/Enphase](https://solarquarter.com/2025/11/06/enphase-iq-batteries-qualify-for-san-diego-community-powers-solar-battery-savings-program); [Solarinsure SDCP](https://solarinsure.com/san-diego-community-power-solar-battery-program)); California's DSGS program, which paid up to about $350 per Powerwall per season in 2024 but whose 2026 funding is contested ([Tesla DSGS](https://www.tesla.com/support/energy/virtual-power-plant/dsgs); [Canary Media](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/virtual-power-plants/california-dsgs-funding-2025-legislation)); SGIP, whose budgets were reported fully reserved or waitlisted in March 2026 and belongs only in upside ([FranklinWH](https://www.franklinwh.com/blog/a-complete-guide-to-california-sgip-program-in-2026); [CPUC SGIP](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/sgip)); in Utah, Rocky Mountain Power's **Wattsmart Battery** program, reported at $400/kW upfront for a four-year commitment plus about $15/kW/year, which for an 11.5 kW Powerwall is **$4,600, or $2,000 if a reported household cap applies** ([KUTV](https://kutv.com/news/eye-on-utah/utah-solar-battery-program-hopes-to-create-virtual-power-plants); [FranklinWH](https://www.franklinwh.com/homeowner-incentives/wattsmart-battery-program)); the **residual value** at a year-6+ FMV buyout or continued grid revenue to year 10; and smaller items Participate also charges, such as non-warranty trip fees up to $350, late fees, and add-on expansion packs (Participate Agreement pp.16, 30–31). **No source confirmed that SDCP, DSGS or Wattsmart pays a third-party owner** or accepts an assignment; Participate handles this by having the customer enroll in their own name and assign the benefit to the owner (pp.19–20), and Tesla is the aggregator in the SDCP agreement Solar Review already uses. Tesla's warranty is 10 years with ~70% capacity retained for self-consumption and backup, but "other applications" are capped at **37,800 kWh** of throughput, so heavy grid dispatch could shorten coverage ([Tesla warranty PDF](https://digitalassets.tesla.com/tesla-contents/image/upload/Powerwall_Warranty_US-EN.pdf)).

### Unit economics: one Powerwall 3, sell through Participate versus own the lease

Assumptions, all labeled. **Solar Review's own figures:** system price $15,000 (mid of $13,950–$16,915), Cosmic redline/install cost $12,500, customer discount 30% so prepay = 70% of price, SDCP $3,375 standard, rep commission 40% of (price − $12,500) on the full system price, company keeps 60%. **My assumptions:** credit basis = $12,500 paid to Cosmic (commissions expensed, not capitalized); credit sold at **80¢ net** after discount, insurance and fees; SDCP evening payments ≈ 10 kWh × 250 weekday evenings × $0.10 ≈ $250/year, owner keeps 70% ≈ $175/year; operations reserve (warranty labor, monitoring, insurance, servicing, possible property tax) **$125/year**; residual shown separately; no time value of money; income tax ignored in the cash lines.

| Line (one system, mid case) | Sell through Participate (today) | Own the lease (prepaid, 30% off) |
|---|---|---|
| Customer pays | $10,500 to Participate (via Cosmic) | $10,500 to Solar Review lessor |
| Paid to Cosmic | n/a (Participate funds Cosmic) | −$12,500 |
| Rep commission (40% × $2,500) | $1,000, paid by Solar Review from its share | −$1,000 |
| Solar Review company margin on day one | **+$1,500** (60% × $2,500), dependent on Participate funding Cosmic | **−$3,000** cash out |
| §48E credit (30% × $12,500 = $3,750), sold at 80¢ | kept by Participate | +$3,000, 3–15 months later |
| Cumulative after credit sale | +$1,500 | **$0** |
| SDCP upfront incentive (if payable to lessor) **[uncertain]** | kept by Participate or passed to customer | +$3,375 |
| SDCP evening payments, 6 yrs × $175 | kept by Participate | +$1,050 |
| Operations reserve, 6 yrs × $125 | none | −$750 |
| **Six-year company result before residual** | **+$1,500** | **+$3,675 with SDCP; +$300 without** |
| Residual at year-6+ FMV buyout **[uncertain]** | none | +$0 to ~$5,000 |

The residual range is a rough illustration: replacement cost × remaining warranty share (~4/10 at year 6 of a 10-year warranty) on $12,500 ≈ $5,000, before degradation; no secondary-market data was found, and a high residual collides with customers' expectation of a near-zero buyout. On the tax side, owning adds roughly **$10,625 of year-one depreciation against ~$10,500 of prepaid rent**, so year-one taxable income is near zero if bonus depreciation applies and the passive rules let the two offset within the same rental activity (CPA item).

The mid case hides how much the price matters. At the low price ($13,950), owning the lease is **−$315 after the credit sale and +$3,060 with SDCP**, versus +$870 through Participate; at the high price ($16,915) it is **+$575 after the credit sale and +$3,950 with SDCP**, versus +$2,649 through Participate (same assumptions; rep commission $580 and $1,766 respectively). Every five points of discount is worth $750 per system at $15,000: offering 25% off instead of 30% moves day-one cash from −$3,000 to −$2,250. Adders swing it further: an energy-community address adds $1,250 of credit (~$1,000 net), CARE customers add $1,350 of SDCP, and a FEOC failure deletes the $3,000 credit entirely, leaving the system roughly **−$3,000 even before operating costs** unless SDCP and the residual rescue it. The structural reason the owned version is tight is that Participate can afford a 30% discount because its credit is figured on a dealer price near $15,000 ($4,500) and it keeps SDCP, grid income and residual; Solar Review's credit is figured on $12,500 ($3,750), so the discount must come out of what used to be Solar Review's dealer margin.

The pilot does not make Solar Review rich; it makes it **independent**. The owned book roughly ties Participate on margin if SDCP flows to the lessor, loses money at 25 units if it does not, and carries all of the residual upside that Participate currently keeps. Fixed costs are the drag: at 100 systems the same $50,000 is $500 per system instead of $2,000, and the credit vintage (~$375,000) clears the ~$250,000 practical floor for better pricing.

## Customer prepayments fund most of each battery; working capital covers the rest

Because the customer pays ~70% up front, the capital need is the **gap plus timing**, not the whole battery. A monthly-payment lease would tie up ~$11,000 per system for years (25 systems ≈ $280,000), which is why a monthly product should wait for real debt (my arithmetic: $15,000 cost less a ~$3,700 credit sale). For the prepaid pilot, the realistic sources are, in order: Solar Review's own operating cash and retained commission income; a small business term loan or equipment line from Wheelhouse Credit Union, which already finances Solar Review customers, possibly with a California IBank guarantee of up to 80% on a CDFI, bank or credit-union loan under $5M (IBank lists distributed storage as a priority sector, though one IBank FAQ says no funding is currently available) ([IBank products](https://cdn.ibank.ca.gov/climate-financing/our-products/); [IBank sectors](https://ibank.ca.gov/climate-financing/sectors-of-focus)); an SBA 7(a) loan, which can fund equipment purchase or leasing but may treat a passive lessor as ineligible unless structured as an Eligible Passive Company ([SOP 50 10 8](https://colemanreport.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/SOP-50-10-8-effective-6.1.2025-Final.pdf)) **[uncertain]**; and friends-and-family or accredited-investor notes under Reg D secured on lease receivables, which need securities counsel. Once a credit buyer signs a forward purchase agreement, a **contracted bridge loan** can advance near 100% of the committed credit value ([Norton Rose/Strafford webinar](https://www.projectfinance.law/media/5921/strafford-tax-equity-bridge-loan-webinar-nrf-_12925.pdf); [Latham](https://www.lw.com/admin/upload/SiteAttachments/Tax-Credit-Transfer-Bridge-Loans-Structuring-Issues-and-Considerations.pdf)). Warehouse lines and securitizations run in the hundreds of millions (Sunrun's April 2026 deal was $584M across 38,706 systems) and are references for scale, not options ([Sunrun](https://investors.sunrun.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/367)). Lenders will underwrite Solar Review's operating business, not a brand-new leasing company with no history, so the borrower is likely the S-corporation even if the batteries sit in a separate lessor entity.

Hold the batteries in a **separate lessor entity** (one per vintage once the book grows) so a single lender default cannot seize everything; PosiGen filed Chapter 11 in November 2025 reportedly to stop its main lender from seizing most customer assets ([pv magazine USA](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2025/12/08/residential-solar-installer-posigen-files-for-bankruptcy/)). Whether that entity is an LLC under the S-corporation, a C-corporation, or a partnership with an outside investor turns on the noncorporate-lessor and passive-activity questions above.

## The risks that matter rank above margin

| Risk | Why it matters | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Powerwall 3 fails FEOC (55% in 2026, rising) | Entire credit disallowed; system goes ~$3,000 negative | Written batch certification before pricing; recheck yearly |

The counterparty risk that prompted this work is real and documented. Sunnova's Chapter 11 left an estimated **~$367M owed to dealers**, who became unsecured creditors while customer contracts moved to a successor servicer ([Octus](https://octus.com/resources/articles/case-summary-sunnova-energy-enters-chapter-11/)); Participate is a privately held company founded in 2022 with LinkedIn listing 2–10 employees and no public capital raise ([LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/company/participate-energy)), and Solar Review's own records show Cosmic's 50% completion payment unpaid while Participate is not funding. Owning the lease converts that receivable risk into asset risk Solar Review controls. It does not eliminate risk: it trades a counterparty's balance sheet for FEOC, tax-structure and servicing risk that sits on Solar Review's own.

## Conclusion

The case for owning battery leases rests less on margin than on control. On Solar Review's own numbers, a prepaid Powerwall lease matches the ~$1,500 per system it earns through Participate only when the SDCP incentive and some residual value land with the lessor, because a vertically integrated lessor earns its credit on Cosmic's $12,500 cost rather than on a retail price; the 30% discount customers now expect has to be paid out of what used to be dealer margin. What owning buys is the removal of a funder that is already late, plus every income stream Participate keeps today, the grid-service split, the residual and future adders. That makes the go/no-go decision unusually concrete: three written answers (a Tesla FEOC certification, an SDCP/Wattsmart payee ruling, and a tax opinion clearing an S-corporation or sibling entity as lessor) decide whether the pilot is worth $75,000–$100,000 of working capital.

A second insight changes the sales pitch. "$0 a month" is a marketing line for a prepaid lease, not a contract structure, and the honest version of it is stronger: customers pay about 70% once, own nothing they must service, and get a defined, appraised buyout from year 6 instead of Participate's undefined one. Pairing that with a fixed grid-revenue split and dealer-friendly timing is the differentiation available to a small lessor, and it is achievable at 25 systems. Scaling beyond that depends on the credit vintage clearing roughly $250,000, which is the point at which a forward credit sale and a contracted bridge loan turn this from a side program into a funding source Solar Review no longer has to ask anyone for.
