# Inland Empire beats the Bay Area for EDDM

**Run the first EDDM pilot in the Inland Empire (western Riverside and San Bernardino counties, e.g. Temecula–Murrieta–Corona–Rancho Cucamonga), not the San Francisco Bay Area. Hold the Bay Area as phase two for PPA/lease sales.** The Inland Empire is the only candidate that combines drive-distance logistics with the freshest pool of stranded solar owners: Freedom Forever (Temecula) was converted to Chapter 7 liquidation in August 2026, and Simply Solar (Rancho Cucamonga) filed Chapter 11 in July 2026. It also sits in SCE territory, where rates are high, and its median home price is mid-range. The Bay Area wins on one thing, the highest electricity rates in the state, which favors PPA/lease sales, but it costs a flight (or a very long drive) for every field visit and has the highest labor costs. **Important limit: no primary dataset (California DG Stats, the CEC county table) could be fetched, so every regional ranking below is inference from rates, utility geography, installer headquarters and statewide trends, not measured install counts.** Cold EDDM at an assumed 0.2–0.4% response is expensive per lead, so the recommended pilot is about **$4,600 for ~10,200 pieces over three touches**, with a hard kill rule. Before spending that, run four free checks, starting with downloading DG Stats and grouping defunct-installer rows by ZIP.

## Ranking: the Inland Empire leads on service, the Bay Area on PPA value

The notes rank regions separately for the two businesses. For diagnostics, repair and battery retrofits, the analyst ranking is Inland Empire first, Bay Area second, LA basin/Orange County third, Sacramento (PG&E side) fourth, Fresno fifth and Ventura/Central Coast last. For PPA/lease new-system sales, it is Bay Area first, Inland Empire second, Sacramento third, Central Valley fourth. Both are **inference only**, built from rate levels, assumed size of the legacy solar base, wildfire-shutoff exposure and home prices (regional demand notes).

The rate evidence is the best-sourced part. PG&E's average residential rate was **40.60 ¢/kWh** as of March 2026 ([PG&E](https://www.pge.com/assets/pge/docs/account/rate-plans/electric-rate-advisory-0326.pdf)), SCE's was **34.5 ¢/kWh** in January 2026 ([SCE](https://www.sce.com/save-money/rates-financing/sce-rate-advisory/20260101)), and municipal utilities are far cheaper: SMUD says its rates are more than 50% below PG&E's ([SMUD](https://www.smud.org/rates)), and a third-party aggregator puts LADWP near 26 ¢ ([OhmSnap](https://www.ohmsnap.com/utility-rate-comparison/california), an estimate). SMUD and LADWP also fall outside DG Stats entirely ([DG Stats FAQ](https://www.californiadgstats.ca.gov/faq/)), so Sacramento city and LA city should be excluded or deprioritized from any mail drop.

Why the Inland Empire tops the service list: Complete Solar's largest California counties are San Bernardino, Los Angeles and San Diego ([Solar.com](https://www.solar.com/installers/complete-solar/), a lead-generation marketplace, so treat as biased), and the 2026 failures are headquartered there. Freedom Forever filed Chapter 11 on April 15, 2026 and converted to Chapter 7 liquidation, with its financing partners resuming stranded installs ([pv magazine USA](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/09/09/freedom-forever-bankruptcy-converted-to-chapter-7-liquidation-key-dates-set/)). Simply Solar LLC of Rancho Cucamonga filed Subchapter V Chapter 11 on July 24, 2026 ([Inforuptcy](https://www.inforuptcy.com/browse-filings/california-central-bankruptcy-court/6:26-bk-16000/bankruptcy-case-simply-solar-llc)), but it said it expects to keep operating, so "closed" is not confirmed and mail copy should not call it defunct. The Inland Empire's September 2026 median home price was **$601,030**, against a statewide median above $900,000 ([C.A.R.](https://blog.car.org/aboutus/mediacenter/newsreleases/2026releases/june2026sales)), which suggests a larger share of homeowners who can still afford a battery retrofit or a lease payment; that is an inference, since ownership and income data were not found.

The Bay Area case is real but narrower. Customers of SunPower, whose historical base is the Bay Area (general knowledge, not sourced in the notes), have reported being out thousands of dollars ([ABC7](https://abc7news.com/post/san-jose-sunpower-solar-panels-bankruptcy-cslb-accusation/15410343/)), and PG&E's rates make a PPA's savings pitch strongest there. Against that, flights are frequent and cheap, with Alaska adding four daily SAN–OAK nonstops in April 2026 ([RoutesOnline](https://www.routesonline.com/airports/6881/oakland-san-francisco-bay-airport/news/299665956/alaska-airlines-announces-san-diego-nonstops-at-oak)), but a field team still needs a local electrician. Bay Area electricians run about **$50–$130 per hour plus a $100–$200 service call** ([Silicon Review](https://thesiliconreview.com/2026/10/bay-area-electrician-rates-2026)); a comparable San Diego rate was not found, so the size of the labor penalty is unquantified. Day trips are feasible; daily field work is not.

## What is verified and what is not

Verified at the level of a vendor or agency page: EDDM pricing and limits, utility rates, the closure and bankruptcy facts for named installers, the California compliance rules, and flight availability. Not verified at all: **how many solar systems, of what age, on which NEM vintage, sit in any county.** DG Stats, the CEC county table and the LBNL data were unreachable from the research environment, so the best statewide fact is only that about 1.6 million households are grandfathered into NEM 1.0/2.0 ([IVN summary](https://ivn.us/posts/how-californians-without-solar-got-stuck-paying-those-who-do-2025-08-19), secondary) and that around late 2019 roughly 60% of IOU NEM systems were NEM 1.0 and 40% NEM 2.0 ([UCR](https://sciencetopolicy.ucr.edu/media/1451/download)). The "Inland Empire has the largest legacy base" claim is a hypothesis resting on installer headquarters and one vendor's county list.

Other open items that change the answer. TPO is now about **45% of California residential installs**, up from 25% in 2022 ([Cali-Energy](https://cali-energy.com/blog/nem-3-solar-market-data-california), a vendor analysis of CPUC data), and 25D ended December 31, 2025, so third-party ownership is the way homeowners can still capture the federal credit through the commercial 48E credit ([NRG Clean Power](https://nrgcleanpower.com/learning-center/federal-solar-tax-credit-updates/)). That supports the PPA pitch, but the 55–70% 2026 TPO share that circulates is an unverified projection. Whether **Axia services Northern California at all is unknown**: it is Qcells' direct-to-consumer arm with a California office in Irvine ([EnergySage](https://www.energysage.com/supplier/26162/axia-solar)), which makes Southern California the safer base for PPA sales regardless of the "all of California" description. Axia's own installer or financing terms (FICO floors, roof and utility rules) could not be found; lender credit minimums conflict between sources (EnFin 620 versus 650+). No regional Tesla-certified installer counts exist in the notes either. Finally, the notes carry no battery-attachment data by region; the 74% statewide new-install attachment rate in 2025 ([LBNL via pv magazine](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/10/03/battery-storage-paired-with-37-of-new-u-s-residential-solar-systems-in-2025/)) excludes retrofits, so it says nothing about retrofit demand.

One structural point matters more than the region choice. **USPS EDDM cannot target solar owners or owner-occupants.** The tool filters routes by age, income and household size only, and every active delivery on a chosen route gets a piece ([USPS](https://www.usps.com/business/every-door-direct-mail.htm); [CATDI](https://www.catdi.com/guides/eddm/targeting-limitations)). Any EDDM drop therefore pays for renters and non-solar homes, and region selection can only be done at ZIP level from DG Stats. The business's own permit data (PermitStack) is address-level and is the stronger targeting asset; vendor material claims targeted lists respond at 0.8–1.8% versus 0.2–0.4% for untargeted EDDM, but that comes from a seller of targeted mail ([directmail.io](https://directmail.io/blog/eddm-vs-targeted-direct-mail-home-services-2026)) and should be tested, not assumed.

## A $4,600 pilot with a kill rule

Postage is **$0.260 per piece** for EDDM Retail (vendor-reported effective July 12, 2026; confirm on USPS Postal Explorer), with a limit of 200 to 5,000 pieces per ZIP per day ([Mailpro](https://www.mailpro.org/post/usps-rate-changes-july-2026/); [USPS](https://www.usps.com/business/every-door-direct-mail.htm)). Vendor calculators put an all-in 10,000-piece postcard drop at roughly **$4,100–$4,700**, about $0.41–$0.47 per piece ([Mailpro calculator](https://mpa-homepage.pages.dev/eddm/cost-calculator/)); printing quotes vary widely ($0.08–$0.32 per piece), so get three, including a Minuteman franchise.

The proposed design, all assumption-based: pick the top 2–3 ZIPs from the DG Stats check, then **6–10 routes totaling about 3,400 homes**, mailed three times about 3–4 weeks apart (about 10,200 pieces, about $4,600 at $0.45). The three-touch, 3–6 week cadence is the common rule of thumb ([Click2Mail](https://blog.click2mail.com/2025/01/16/the-three-touch-rule-building-an-effective-direct-mail-sequence/); [Lob](https://www.lob.com/blog/marketing-postcard-frequency-how-often-is-too-often)), not a solar-tested result. Split routes between two creatives, each with its own tracked phone number and QR code: A, "Is your solar installer still in business? Free system check," and B, "Check your solar production and bill." Keep one offer and one call to action. Run time is 10–12 weeks including two to three weeks of response tail.

Expected results, all guesses: at 0.3% response, about 31 responses; at an optimistic 1%, about 100. If 30–50% become held appointments and 20–30% of those close, that is roughly 3–12 sales. At an assumed $1,500 gross profit per sale, **about three sales break even**; at $500 (diagnostic fee only), about nine. Cold EDDM cost per held appointment likely lands at **$250–$750** (derived from the cited $175–$350 per lead at 0.2–0.4% response). Success threshold: cost per held appointment at or below about $300 and at least one sale per $1,500 spent; **kill or retarget if response is under 0.2% after touch two.** Typical solar-lead alternatives run about $110–$206 per lead ([ActiveProspect](https://activeprospect.com/blog/how-much-do-solar-leads-cost/)), so EDDM only wins if the orphaned-installer hook beats generic response rates; no benchmark exists for that hook.

Compliance must be settled before printing. California requires the contractor license number in all advertising (Business & Professions Code §7030.5, with civil penalties of $100 to $1,000 for a first offense) ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-bpc/division-3/chapter-9/article-2/section-7030-5/); [CSLB](https://www2.cslb.ca.gov/Newsletter/2019-Spring/3-Advertising-Rules.asp)). Because the operator sells through a licensed partner (Cosmic), the partner's name and license number likely must appear on the mailer; confirm with counsel or CSLB. Avoid savings guarantees and any claim of manufacturer affiliation. Call-ins need an all-party recording disclosure (Penal Code §632, up to $5,000 per violation) ([Steptoe](https://www.steptoe.com/en/news-publications/call-recording-cases-on-hold-court-of-appeal-rings-in-new-year-with-landmark-cipa-decision.html)). Working outside San Diego also means city business registrations: a CSLB license is statewide, but cities may require proof of local business tax before issuing a permit ([CSLB Building Officials Guide](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/Resources/GuidesAndPublications/BuildingOfficialsGuide.pdf)); one example is Gardena's roughly $150 fee for out-of-city A/B contractors ([City of Gardena](https://cityofgardena.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/CONTRACTOR-LICENSE-GUIDELINE-2026.pdf)). Permit friction itself is lower than it used to be, since SolarAPP+ is mandated for most jurisdictions ([NREL](https://www.nrel.gov/docs/fy22osti/81603.pdf)), though panel upgrades and utility interconnection still drive timelines.

## Checks to run before spending a dollar

First, **download the DG Stats interconnected-project-sites file and data key** ([downloads](https://www.californiadgstats.ca.gov/downloads)); confirm whether it lists installer name and ZIP or city, then count rows by defunct-installer name and by interconnection year per ZIP and county. This one check could replace most of the inference in this report. Second, **query PermitStack for the target ZIPs** (San Jose and Sacramento, plus the Inland Empire cities) and see how many addresses come back for SunPower/Complete Solar, Titan, Sunnova, Freedom Forever, Sunworks and Simply Solar; open permit data is patchy, with San Jose publishing a CKAN portal ([listing](https://www.firecrawl.dev/alexandria/agents/providers/data-sanjoseca-gov)) and Shovels as a possible paid statewide source at an unverified $599–$999 per month ([API Evangelist](https://apis.apievangelist.com/store/shovels-api)). Third, **ask Axia directly** about its service area (Northern California or only Southern?), its crew versus subcontractor model, and whether it supports a referral partner with a mailing program. Fourth, **ask for 3 printing quotes** and confirm the EDDM rate on Postal Explorer, then build the mailer's license and disclosure language with a CSLB-literate reviewer. Lower priority: confirm the AB 942 outcome and the NEM 20-year start rule before any NEM-based copy ([SolarReviews](https://www.solarreviews.com/blog/california-ab942-break-solar-promises)), pull Tesla's installer locator by ZIP for each target, and use the CEC county table to rank counties by system count.

## Conclusion

The question assumed the Bay Area was the default and the rest were alternatives; the evidence flips that. A San Diego owner's binding constraint is reach, and the strongest orphan-and-service audience in the state sits two hours north in the Inland Empire, with Freedom Forever and Simply Solar failures that line up directly with the business's PTO-completion and repair offer. The Bay Area's PG&E rates are a real advantage for PPA sales, but a PPA does not require a Bay Area mailing: PPA leads can come from the same Southern California drops once Axia's service area is confirmed, and the Bay Area can be tested later with a small mailing to a few high-rate ZIPs.

The most useful finding is about method. EDDM's untargeted routes mean a cheap pilot is an experiment on a message, not a precise market measurement, so judge it on cost per held appointment and use the business's own address-level permit data to decide whether to move to targeted mail. If a free DG Stats pull shows the Inland Empire lacks older, installer-heavy ZIPs, or shows Santa Clara and the East Bay dominate, the ranking should change; that check costs nothing and should come first.
